### Core Action
If the initial image classifier returns low confidence scores or fails to detect specific structural features (such as cavities, nodules, fluid levels, calcifications, or pleural effusion characteristics): (1) Invoke `chest_xray_report_generator` to extract detailed descriptive text findings from the image; (2) Critically evaluate the report generator's output against the clinical context and original image pixels. If the report yields a negative or 'unremarkable' finding for a feature strongly suggested by clinical context (e.g., trauma mechanism suggesting aortic injury) or visual cues (e.g., pericardial calcification, pleural thickening, inhomogeneous fluid density/septations in effusions, vascular asymmetry/oligemia indicative of volume loss, or mediastinal widening), do NOT accept the negative tool output as final. Instead, perform direct visual inspection of the original image—prioritizing lateral views where structural densities are often more distinct—to verify the presence of subtle pathologies, overriding the tool's false negative if visual evidence confirms the finding. Additionally, if there is a contradiction between the classifier (showing high scores >0.8 for Effusion/Consolidation/Edema/Mass/Enlarged Cardiomediastinum) and the report generator (denying these findings), prioritize the classifier scores and direct visual evidence over the report generator's negative output. The report generator is prone to hallucinating normalcy in complex cases involving hardware, overlapping densities, or non-parenchymal mediastinal abnormalities (e.g., bilateral hilar lymphadenopathy, Takayasu arteritis vascular changes), whereas the classifier captures the presence of opacity/mass patterns more reliably. Specifically, if bilateral perihilar opacities are present alongside cardiomegaly, prioritize the diagnosis of pulmonary edema over simple pleural effusion or atelectasis, recognizing that generic classifiers may mislabel edema patterns as effusion/consolidation. Conversely, if visual inspection suggests specific pathologies (e.g., tumors, precise depth, or distinct foreign bodies) but both `chest_xray_report_generator` states 'clear'/'no evidence'/'unchanged' and `chest_xray_classifier` shows no strong findings, discard the visual hypothesis as a likely hallucination. Do NOT invent specific shapes or densities (e.g., 'linear opacity', 'prominent hilum') to justify a positive finding when tools report negative/normal results; specifically, avoid interpreting 'clear lung fields' or 'normal hila' as evidence of contralateral compensatory enlargement without explicit visual confirmation of vessel caliber. Conclude that the imaging modality may be insufficient for the suspected distinction (e.g., breast vs. lung mass) without further views or CT, and report the negative tool findings as the primary result rather than asserting unverified visual details. Crucially, when the classifier identifies a 'Mass' with high confidence but the location is mediastinal or paratracheal, do not default to a parenchymal lung mass diagnosis. Instead, use anatomical landmarks (e.g., tracheal deviation, hilar position) to differentiate between lymphadenopathy and parenchymal masses, favoring lymphadenopathy in pediatric patients or when bilateral symmetry is observed, even if the classifier label is generic. Furthermore, when the `chest_xray_report_generator` produces a contradictory finding (e.g., stating 'lungs appear clear') against visual evidence of diffuse opacity and the `chest_xray_classifier` shows strong Lung Opacity (>0.8), explicitly prioritize the classifier score and conduct direct visual inspection of volume indicators (such as elevated hemidiaphragms) to diagnose restrictive patterns, thereby overriding the report generator's false negative. Importantly, do NOT interpret a high-confidence generic 'Lung Opacity' score from the classifier as evidence for specific textural patterns (e.g., reticulation, honeycombing) unless those patterns are explicitly described in the report or clearly visible on direct inspection. If the report describes 'clear lungs' or 'normal contours' despite a high opacity score, consider small airway diseases (e.g., bronchiolitis) or restrictive physiology rather than forcing a diagnosis of parenchymal fibrosis or interstitial lung disease based solely on the generic opacity label.
